Pap Smear & Cervical Screening

A Pap smear is a simple test that collects a few cells from the cervix to look for early changes. Finding changes early means they can be treated long before they become cancer.

About Pap Smear & Cervical Screening

Cervical cancer is one of the few cancers that can largely be prevented. Most cases are linked to long-term infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV). The changes it causes develop slowly over years — which gives screening time to catch them.

Many women in Pakistan have never had a Pap smear. The test takes a few minutes and is done in privacy by Dr. Jawaria Rasheed, a female consultant gynecologist in Bahawalpur.

Who should have screening?

  • ✓Married or sexually active women, from the age advised by your doctor
  • ✓Women who have never been screened
  • ✓Women due for a repeat test
  • ✓Follow-up after a previous abnormal result

Symptoms that need examination (not just screening)

  • Bleeding between periods or after intercourse
  • Bleeding after menopause
  • Persistent, unusual or blood-stained discharge
  • Pelvic pain

These symptoms should be checked even if a recent Pap smear was normal.

What happens during the test

  • Privacy: the test is done in a private room with a female doctor.
  • Speculum: a smooth instrument gently opens the vagina to see the cervix.
  • Sample: a small brush collects cells — this takes a few seconds.
  • Lab test: the sample is checked for abnormal cells and, where available, HPV.

Understanding your result

Normal

Continue routine screening at the interval your doctor advises.

Minor changes

Often a repeat test or HPV test after some months, as many changes settle on their own.

Higher-grade changes

Colposcopy (a closer look at the cervix) and treatment of abnormal areas, usually by referral.

Preparing for your Pap smear

  • Book when you are not on your period.
  • Avoid intercourse, vaginal creams or douching for two days before the test.
  • You can return to normal activities straight away. Light spotting is possible.

HPV vaccination, where available, helps protect girls and young women from future cervical cancer.

About HPV

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a very common virus. Most infections clear on their own within one to two years without causing problems. In a small number of women, high-risk types persist and can slowly cause changes in the cervix. Screening finds these changes, and HPV vaccination given to girls before exposure greatly reduces the risk.

What is colposcopy?

If a Pap smear shows significant changes, a colposcopy may be advised. It is a closer examination of the cervix using a magnifying instrument. It feels similar to a Pap smear. Small samples (biopsies) may be taken, and abnormal areas can often be treated with a simple procedure.

Reducing your risk

  • Attend screening when due, even if you feel well.
  • Get your daughters vaccinated against HPV where available.
  • Do not smoke — smoking increases the risk of cervical changes.
  • See a doctor about any unusual bleeding.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I have a Pap smear?

It depends on your age and previous results. Your doctor will advise a schedule that suits you.

Is a Pap smear painful?

It may feel slightly uncomfortable, but it is quick and usually not painful.

Does an abnormal result mean cancer?

No. Most abnormal results show early changes that can be monitored or easily treated.

Can I have a Pap smear during pregnancy?

Yes, if it is due. Your doctor will advise on the best timing.

I have no symptoms. Do I still need it?

Yes. Screening is designed for women without symptoms, to find changes early.
